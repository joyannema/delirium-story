Sometimes the Body Heals Before the Mind Does

About one in four older adults develops delirium after surgery. It is often missed, mistaken for ordinary drowsiness, and for some patients its effects last for years.

Two summers ago, I volunteered at the Montreal Neurological Institute doing what the hospital calls friendly visits: sitting with patients who had just come out of surgery and keeping them company.

Some of them didn't know where they were. Some couldn't remember why they were in the hospital at all. Their bodies had made it through the operation. Their minds hadn't caught up.

I didn't have a name for it then. It's called postoperative delirium: a sudden disturbance in attention and awareness after surgery, and one of the most common complications older patients face. The form that's easiest to miss isn't agitation or shouting. It's withdrawal: a patient who seems sleepy, slow, a little "off."

Picture 100 adults over 65 going into surgery.
Across 19 studies of more than 3,500 older surgical patients, researchers found that about 24 in 100 developed delirium afterward.
But being delirious and being diagnosed are different things. In one Cleveland Clinic study, an advanced practice nurse screening patients on a postoperative ward found 24 cases of delirium.
The ward's nurses had identified four. Most of the missed cases were the quiet, hypoactive kind.

That ward isn't unusual. Where staff don't screen every patient with a standard checklist, a review for anesthesiologists estimated that delirium is missed in roughly 60 to 80 percent of cases.

What it looks like on the ward

At the Neuro, the nursing team cares for neurosurgery and stroke patients ranging from 18 to 99 years old. When my Revaive teammates and I went back to sit down with two of the unit's assistant nurse managers, they described delirium less as a statistic than as a pattern they recognize.

It often surfaces at a transition, they said: when patients leave the intensive care unit for a regular floor, with new rooms, new faces and new noises. In older patients, acute delirium can look like restlessness: more confusion, trying to climb out of bed, being awake the whole night.

Families are often the first to sense it. Their most common words, the nurses said, are "That's not them."

But even for experienced staff, one question is hard to answer at the bedside: is this delirium, or a cognitive problem the patient already had? Telling them apart depends on knowing what the patient was like before, which is exactly what families know and staff often don't.

Why we built a tool for this

Two summers ago, it was just me, in one room, with one patient.

When I joined the AI4Good Lab at Mila in 2026, I pitched an idea built on those visits, and it was one of four projects selected. With three teammates, I spent the program building Revaive around the two gaps in this story: hospitals have no systematic way to flag who's at risk before surgery, and the cognitive stimulation that helps prevent delirium is hard to deliver every day, because nurses, volunteers and family can't always be there.

That's why we went back to the Neuro before demo day. We didn't want to build it for the nurses; we wanted to build it with them.

Pillar 1 · Detection

Risk prediction

Tree-based models (XGBoost and random forest) built on clinician-reviewed features flag patients at high risk of delirium at admission. Because a missed case is costly in medicine, we tuned them for recall: catching as many at-risk patients as possible.

Pillar 2 · Support

Revi, an AI voice agent

Revi runs short daily voice sessions that give patients cognitive stimulation, then turns them into a recovery score nurses can track, built from how coherent the patient's speech is, how varied their vocabulary is, and how quickly they speak and respond. It's meant to support human care, not replace it.

How a check-in goes (from our demo)

ReviHello! I'm Revi, and I'm here to support you today. Is there anything on your mind you'd like to share?
PatientI really love apples. They're my favourite fruit.
ReviApples are such a delicious choice! If you had 20 apples and gave away 7, how many would you have left?
PatientI think I'd have 2. I'm honestly not feeling very well right now.
ReviI want to make sure you're okay. I'll let your nurse know.

Each answer feeds the recovery score, and when the patient says they don't feel well, Revi hands off to a nurse instead of trying to handle it alone.

The bigger picture

What the nurses described at the Neuro shows up in research around the world.

The risk depends heavily on the operation

Not every surgery carries the same risk. A 2023 review that pooled 90 studies of patients 65 and older found rates ranged from about one in ten for urologic procedures to nearly one in three for heart surgery.

Who is most at risk

Age is the biggest single factor: the risk climbs past 65. But what a patient brings into the operating room matters too. Existing memory problems or dementia, depression, poor vision or hearing, and regular use of sedatives like benzodiazepines all raise the odds. So does what happens after: pain, low oxygen, infection, and long stretches on a breathing tube.

Many of these are visible before surgery begins, which is why researchers argue hospitals could flag high-risk patients at admission and watch them most closely.

Source: StatPearls, "Postoperative Delirium" (NCBI Bookshelf, 2023).

It doesn't always go away

Delirium was long treated as temporary, something that clears once the anesthesia wears off. Research over the past two decades has complicated that picture. In a German study that followed hip surgery patients aged 60 and older for about three years after discharge, the gap was stark:

53.8%
of surviving patients who had delirium showed cognitive impairment about 38 months later
4.4%
of patients who did not have delirium showed the same

Source: Hip surgery cohort, n = 200 (Bickel et al., 2008).

The evidence on deaths is more mixed. A 2019 study of orthopedic patients found higher mortality three years later among those who'd had delirium. But a 2020 study of elective noncardiac surgery found no link to five-year mortality once researchers accounted for how sick patients already were. Delirium may be a warning sign of frailty as much as a cause of harm, and researchers are still untangling the two.

Many hospitals don't check

Spotting delirium requires looking for it. A study of nearly 98,000 surgical patients aged 75 and older, published in 2026, found that only about half were screened after their operations. More than 94 percent of patients at hospitals certified in a geriatric surgery quality program were screened, compared with 53 percent elsewhere.

What helps

The encouraging part: delirium is often preventable. In randomized trials, simple non-drug measures, such as getting patients moving, making sure they have their glasses and hearing aids, reminding them of the date and place, and keeping their minds engaged, have cut delirium rates by 30 to 50 percent.

On the Neuro's unit, some of that is low-tech: whiteboards in patients' rooms showing the date and where they are, and a structured daily routine. When that isn't enough, staff may turn to medication. The hard part, everywhere, is doing the non-drug measures consistently on busy wards.

What families can doYou know what your parent is normally like, and staff don't. If they seem unusually sleepy, confused about the day, or "not themselves," tell the care team, especially about the quiet form, which staff miss most.

Methods & limitations

Incidence figures come from published systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Rates vary widely between studies depending on the patients and the screening tool used. The Cleveland Clinic figures come from a single hospital unit and illustrate under-recognition; they are not a national rate.

Revaive's risk models were trained on MIMIC-IV, de-identified records from one Boston hospital. We don't report performance numbers here: after the program we found issues with how our training dataset was put together that we haven't yet resolved. Revaive is a student prototype; it has not been validated on other patients and is not used for clinical decisions.
